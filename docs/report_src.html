Throughline

Your weekly step-back.
Author: Kristine Feng, Yale School of Management
GitHub repo: github.com/kristinefeng/throughline
App: runs locally from a fresh clone in about three minutes (README → “Run it”). No hosted link.
Model: gpt-6-luna through the course Portkey key

Throughline is your weekly step-back: five minutes to see the bigger picture of your life, in your own words. You save small moments as you go, a line and a photo. When a week blurs together, Zoom Out shows what it held, what's quietly slipping and how you've grown, quoting your own words as evidence. You shape the story: adjust what doesn't fit, and decide where it goes.

2Motivation

Problem statement. People in a demanding season of life capture more of it than ever, yet rarely step back to see what it adds up to. The weeks blur together, and they can't easily tell whether the way they spend their days still matches what they care about, until they've already drifted.
How might we give busy people five minutes a week to see the bigger picture of their lives, in their own words?
The use case. Who: people in a demanding season of life (MBA recruiting, a first job, a new baby). When: the end of a week that blurred together. The job: “Help me step back and see what my life actually looked like, and whether it matches what I care about.”

Who has this problem. People in fast, demanding seasons of life: students, early-career professionals, new parents, anyone in a transition. The person I had in mind is a classmate in the middle of MBA recruiting. Her phone is full of photos from a year she'll remember as one of the best of her life. Yet most weeks she feels behind, and she can't tell whether the way she's spending her days still matches what she came to business school for. Life doesn't come with a handbook. Most of us also don't have a regular moment to step back and look at the one we're writing.

Why it matters. The raw material for perspective has never been more plentiful, or more neglected:

  • We capture more than ever. Google Photos alone holds over 9 trillion photos and videos for more than 1.5 billion monthly users (Google, May 2025, company-reported).
  • We underestimate the value of looking back. People consistently underestimate how much they'll enjoy rediscovering ordinary moments later. In one study, only 27% chose to write about a recent conversation, yet 58% chose to reread such an account a month later (Zhang, Kim, Brooks, Gino & Norton, Psychological Science, 2014).
  • Values and daily life drift apart. In APA's 2025 Stress in America survey (N = 3,199), 92% of U.S. adults named relationships as a key source of meaning, yet 54% felt isolated at least sometimes.
  • Young adults are stretched. In 2023, 34% of 18–34-year-olds rated their stress 8–10 out of 10 (APA, N = 3,185).
  • Reflection helps, modestly. A meta-analysis of 146 randomized studies found a small but real benefit from expressive writing (Frattaroli, Psychological Bulletin, 2006). Recalling positive memories after a stressor blunted the stress hormone response in a lab study (Speer & Delgado, Nature Human Behaviour, 2017).

How people deal with it today, and what that costs.

  • Photo apps resurface pictures by date, but they don't know why a moment mattered.
  • Journals capture thoughts, but connecting entries across months means hours of rereading. Rereading Maya's 18 short entries alone takes about 10 minutes (Section 7), and almost nobody does it.
  • Goal apps add streaks and scores: more pressure, less perspective.
  • Coaching and therapy help, but they're expensive and not built for a weekly “how is my life going?”
  • ChatGPT: some people paste their journal into it. “Relationships and personal reflection” is about 1.9% of all ChatGPT messages (OpenAI/NBER Working Paper 34255, 2025). That's a lot of reflection happening in a tool that doesn't know your values, can't see your photos, and can't show you which of your words it is relying on.

The cost is not dollars. It's the quiet frustration of a full life that feels like a blur, and the risk of drifting from what matters without noticing until much later.

Product goal. After five minutes with Throughline, a person should be able to say: “I feel a little more connected to myself, and I have a clearer perspective on my life.”

The honest picture:

  • Finding patterns across entries: Rosebud, Day One, Mindsera and Stoic all do this.
  • Linking back to entries: Rosebud and Day One do this, and ChatGPT shows which chats it drew on.
  • Remembering feedback: Rosebud and ChatGPT both do this in some form.
  • Resurfacing photos: Apple and Google do this well.

So none of these features is new on its own. What no product I reviewed documents is the combination Throughline is built around, and each part changes the experience:

  1. Evidence you can check, checked by code. Every observation comes with your exact words, and the app removes anything it can't find in the entry it cites. It can be wrong about what something means, but it can't put words in your mouth. For an app that tells people things about their own lives, that's the difference between “interesting” and “trustworthy”.
  2. Judged against values you wrote. Throughline doesn't decide what a good life looks like or infer your personality. It holds your moments up against your own North Star, and that is how it can notice a quiet tension: health is on your list, but running disappeared in September.
  3. You shape the story, and it listens. On every pattern you can say it resonates, is partly true, or isn't you. The Mirror adjusts instead of insisting, and the next Zoom Out reads what you said.
  4. Your values change only with your consent. The AI can suggest rewording a value, but nothing changes until you click Accept.
  5. A mirror, not a coach. There are no streaks, scores or reminders. It's built to be something you can open on a hard week without being told you're behind.

4The agents

Throughline has three AI agents and four checks written in plain code. The agents do the work that needs judgment: reading a moment, finding a thread across months, and saying it well. The code does the work that must be reliable every time: catching crisis language, holding back injected instructions, verifying quotes, and enforcing the budget.

AgentWhat it doesTools it can use
Memory KeeperWhen a moment is saved: it writes a one-line summary and tags (themes, values, feelings, people, places), and describes the photo if there is one. This is the only place images are sent to the model. On Zoom Out with a specific question: it searches the journal in an agent loop, deciding what to search for and reading the results, then returns the 5–8 moments it picked.search_memories(query, since, until), recent_memories(n). Both are read-only and limited to the current person's space.
Pattern FinderReads the moments next to the person's North Star and names 1–3 throughlines. Each one is labelled living it, a quiet tension, how you've grown or gone quiet, and must be backed by 2–4 quotes copied word for word from different entries.get_memory(id) to read more, and get_past_feedback() to see what the person said about earlier reflections.
MirrorTurns the verified throughlines into a 90–150 word reflection and one open question. In Reflect Back, it listens to the person's response, adjusts instead of insisting it was right, and may suggest one change to their North Star.propose_north_star_update(...). This only stages a suggestion card. Nothing changes until the person clicks Accept.

How the agents are organized

A fixed pipeline, not a boss agent. Every Zoom Out needs the same steps in the same order, so the hand-offs are set in code: find moments → find patterns → verify quotes → write the reflection. That is cheaper than having a manager agent decide the order each time, and it is predictable. It also lets plain code check each agent's output before the next one starts. The grounding check sits between the Pattern Finder and the Mirror for exactly this reason: the Mirror only ever sees patterns whose evidence has been verified.

Four ways to zoom out. This week is the default and the main use case: the newest week is the focus, and the last three months are read too so the week is seen in context. This season and The whole story widen the lens, and Ask your own question lets the person set the focus.

A router decides whether to search. I first built Zoom Out to always run the Memory Keeper's search. My cost experiment (Section 5) showed that for a journal of fewer than about 40 moments, reading every moment is cheaper and gives richer evidence. So the router (plain code) sends small journals straight to the Pattern Finder. It uses the Memory Keeper's search when the person asks their own question, or once the journal is too long to read in full.

Feedback closes the loop. When a person says a throughline is “partly” true or “not me”, the response is saved. The next Pattern Finder run reads it with get_past_feedback. In the run traced below, the Pattern Finder did exactly that.

Which model and why

All three agents use gpt-6-luna, the only model on the course key, through Portkey's OpenAI-compatible gateway and the Responses API (luna requires it for tool calling). Luna turned out to be a good fit, not just the only option:

  • It reads photos. I tested this first with a generated image before designing the Memory Keeper around it.
  • It caches repeated prompt prefixes, billed at a tenth of the input price.
  • It's cheap. GPT-6 Astra is exactly 100× the price on every line.

I set reasoning effort per agent. The Pattern Finder gets medium, because noticing a quiet tension across months is the hard part. The Memory Keeper and Mirror get low, since summarizing and writing in a set voice don't need it. Each agent also has a hard request limit: 2 requests to index a moment or write a reflection, 3 for Reflect Back, and 6 for search or pattern finding.

One real run, step by step

This is a real run on the demo journal of “Maya”, a fictional MBA student with 18 moments saved between April and October 2026. Earlier in the session she had zoomed out on “this season” and told the Mirror that one throughline was only partly true (Figures 4–5). She then chose Ask your own question and typed “When have I felt most like myself this year?”

  1. Safety and routing (code, $0). No recent entry needs the care pause, and the budget has room. Because this is a custom question, the router sends it to the Memory Keeper to search.
  2. Memory Keeper ($0.0005, 2 requests, 3,578 input tokens). It ran three searches of its own: "felt like myself authenticity joy adventure growth", "connection family friends belonging" and "health energy outdoors movement". Then it checked recent_memories(10). It picked 7 moments across the year: the watercolor evening, a Saturday off, a last-minute beach trip, the scallion-pancake dinner, an unplanned hike, a dawn run, and a porch dinner.
  3. Pattern Finder ($0.0009, 2 requests, 5,949 input tokens, 2,799 of them cached). It first called get_past_feedback() and got back Maya's earlier correction: “Recruiting isn't the enemy, I chose it. What I really miss is my Sunday calls with Nai Nai.” It then proposed 3 throughlines with quotes.
  4. Grounding check (code, $0). Every quote was compared with the entry it cites. All of them matched, so nothing was dropped. (When something doesn't match, the dropped quote and the reason are listed here.)
  5. Mirror ($0.0002, 1 request, 1,132 input tokens). It wrote the reflection “A season of open days and long tables” and the question “What do the unplanned hours, late dinners, and time with paint have in common when you feel most like yourself?”

Total: $0.0016 and about 13 seconds. The app shows all of this to the person under “How this was made” (Figure 3).

Per user and at scale

A typical active month is 20 moments saved, 4 Zoom Outs (one of them a custom question) and 4 Reflect Backs. A heavy user does three times that.

The same workload on GPT-6 Astra would cost 100× as much: about $1 per user per month, or $10,100 a month at 10,000 users. At luna prices, tokens are no longer the main cost of running Throughline for an organization. Hosting and photo storage would be: 20 photos a month at about 250 KB each is 5 MB per user per month, or 50 GB a month across 10,000 users.

What I did to keep cost down

  1. Read photos once. The Memory Keeper describes each photo when it's saved. Every later step reads that one sentence instead of the image.
  2. Shrink photos before the model sees them. I resize them to 768 px on the long side. In a direct comparison on the same photo, this cut input from 1,872 to 1,192 tokens (−36%).
  3. Put the stable text first, so it's cached. The long instructions and the North Star go first, and the moments follow in date order. Repeat runs reuse the cached prefix, so 41% of a typical Zoom Out's input is billed at a tenth of the price.
  4. Route small journals past search (see the experiment below).
  5. Set reasoning effort per agent, and cap requests per agent, per run ($0.05) and per day ($1.00).

Did cutting cost hurt quality? The evidence

Photo size: no. The two descriptions of the same trail photo were nearly identical. At 768 px: “A narrow rocky trail winds through dense green trees and moss-covered boulders in soft, overcast light, creating a quiet, adventurous mood.” At 1,600 px: “A rocky path winds between mossy boulders and dense green trees in soft, overcast light, creating a quiet, adventurous mood.”

Search versus reading everything: a surprise, and a design change. I assumed agentic search would be the cheap option. To test it, I ran Zoom Out on Maya's full 18-moment journal three times each way. I bypassed Portkey's response cache so the trials were independent; with the cache on, repeated identical requests came back byte-for-byte identical, which I only noticed because my first three trials matched exactly.

Reading everything was 35% cheaper and cited more moments, with no loss in grounding. The whole journal is the same prompt every time, so almost all of it comes from the cache. Search picks different moments each time, which defeats the cache, and it adds the Memory Keeper's own requests. So I changed the design: journals up to 40 moments (about 10,000 tokens) are read in full, and search is used for custom questions and longer journals. Retrieval still matters at scale. Reading everything grows linearly with the journal and would eventually hit long-context pricing, which doubles above 272K tokens per request.

6Safety

Throughline holds some of the most personal data a person has. I focused on four risks that actually matter for this app, built a defense for each, and tested every defense. The 16 automated tests run without an API key in under a second (python -m pytest backend/tests), and a live attack test runs against the real model.

Agent malfunction and privacy

  • Wrong action: no agent can delete, send, or change anything. The only write (a North Star change) is staged and needs a click.
  • Looping: request limits stop it. A stopped run returns a clear message and still shows its trace.
  • Provider errors: they are caught and explained, never shown as a crash.
  • Privacy: everything is stored locally in data/, which is never committed. Uploaded photos are re-encoded, which strips location metadata. Any moment can be deleted together with its photo, and the AI only receives the moments a Zoom Out needs.

7Impact

Throughline doesn't save anyone money, and the time it saves isn't the main point. Its value is making it possible to step back at all on an ordinary, busy day. So I measured it four ways, and I say where each number comes from.

1. Real people tried it

2. Time: from ten minutes of rereading to under thirty seconds

Maya's 18 moments total 2,436 words. At an average adult silent-reading speed of 238 words per minute (Brysbaert, 2019, Journal of Memory and Language), simply rereading them takes about 10 minutes. That's before the harder work of noticing that running disappeared in September, or that the watercolors were mentioned three times before she finally painted. A Zoom Out took 10–30 seconds in every run I logged, and it hands back the patterns with the quotes behind them. This is a careful estimate from my own data, not a measured user time; the pilot in Section 8 would measure it directly.

3. Trustworthiness: every quote a person sees is real

Across every Zoom Out I logged, the grounding check confirmed every quote shown to the person. In the six independent experiment runs, every quote the model produced was accurate. In one earlier run, the check caught and dropped a quote that didn't match its entry, which is exactly the case it exists for. When I deliberately fed it invented quotes in the tests, it removed them. For a product that tells people things about their own lives, this is the impact I care most about: Throughline can be wrong about an interpretation, and the person can say so, but it can't put words in their mouth.

4. Reach: affordable for a whole organization

At about 1¢ per active user per month in model costs (Section 5), a business school, an employer's wellbeing program, or a university counseling center's self-help shelf could offer Throughline to 10,000 people for about $100 a month. Hosting and storage would come on top. Before Throughline, offering everyone a personal, evidence-backed reflection on their own life wasn't possible at any price. The closest substitute is a coach or counselor, who brings judgment and care that Throughline doesn't claim to replace.

8Limits and next steps

What doesn't work yet

  • It's single-user and local. There are no accounts, no sync, and no phone app. Capture has to happen at a computer, and that is the biggest gap given that most moments start as phone photos.
  • The evidence of impact is thin. It comes from a small number of short first sessions. I can't yet show whether Throughline changes anything over weeks, which is the claim that matters.
  • The care check is a phrase list. It's deliberately cautious and deterministic, but it is not a clinical screening tool, and it will miss indirect expressions of distress.
  • Search is keyword-based (BM25 over the AI's tags). The course key allows one model, so there are no embeddings.
  • The provider's content filter can block a whole Zoom Out if one released entry reads like a jailbreak. Throughline fails safely, but it doesn't yet say which moment caused it.
  • The demo journal is fictional, written to contain realistic patterns. Real journals are messier.

With another month

  1. Phone capture: share a photo from the camera roll straight into Throughline, with an optional voice note. Capture has to be as easy as taking the photo.
  2. A four-week pilot with 15–20 people, with weekly Zoom Outs. I'd measure the same three items each week, plus whether people act on their reflection question.
  3. A “Your week in perspective” digest: one gentle Sunday reflection, opt-in, with no streaks.
  4. An evaluation set for pattern quality: human-rated throughlines, so I can compare prompts and models on quality, not just cost.
  5. Encrypted sync and accounts, keeping the rule that nothing is shared without consent.
  6. Pinpoint filtered moments: when the provider blocks a run, find the responsible moment and hold it back automatically.
